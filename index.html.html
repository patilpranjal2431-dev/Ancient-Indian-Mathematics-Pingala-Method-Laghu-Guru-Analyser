Shloka to Laghu-Guru Conversion

In Sanskrit and Marathi literature, poetic compositions known as shlokas are built using fundamental sound units called akshara (syllables). Each akshara is a unit of pronunciation and plays a key role in maintaining the rhythm and structure of the verse.

The study of these rhythmic patterns is part of an ancient discipline known as Chandas (prosody), which focuses on how syllables are arranged in a systematic and mathematical manner to form poetic meters.

Every syllable (akshara) in a shloka is classified into two types:

Laghu (L) — a short syllable
Guru (G) — a long syllable

A syllable is considered Laghu when it contains short vowels such as अ, इ, उ, ऋ or matras like ि, ु, ृ, े, ो.

A syllable is considered Guru when it contains long vowels such as आ, ई, ऊ, ऐ, औ or matras like ा, ी, ू, ै, ौ.

The conversion must be performed syllable by syllable and not word by word.

Spaces are preserved using double spacing.

This helps identify classical Sanskrit meters such as Anushtubh, Trishtubh, and Jagati.

Thus, this conversion is both linguistic and mathematical, transforming sound into structured symbolic patterns.

Nāṣṭa Method

Nāṣṭa is a classical algorithm used to convert numbers into Laghu-Guru sequences.

Odd → 0 → (n+1)/2
Even → 1 → n/2

Example:

37 → 0 → 19
19 → 0 → 10
10 → 1 → 5
5 → 0 → 3
3 → 0 → 2
2 → 1 → 1

Final sequence: 001001

This method represents early combinatorics.

Uddiṣṭa Method

Uddiṣṭa is the reverse of Nāṣṭa.

Start from 1.

1 → ×2
0 → ×2 −1

Ignore trailing zeros.

Example:

1010100 → 10101

1 → 2
0 → 3
1 → 6
0 → 11
1 → 22

Final result: 22

This shows early algorithmic thinking.

Pingala and Ancient Indian Mathematics

Pingala was an ancient Indian scholar who is believed to have lived around 200 BCE. He is one of the earliest known thinkers to apply mathematical principles to linguistic and poetic analysis.

He is best known for his work Chandaḥśāstra, a foundational text on Sanskrit prosody (Chandas), which studies the structure and rhythm of poetic meters.

In this work, Pingala analyzed how Laghu (short) and Guru (long) syllables combine to form structured patterns in Sanskrit verses.

To systematically study these patterns, he introduced important algorithms such as Nāṣṭa and Uddiṣṭa.

The Nāṣṭa method converts numbers into sequences of Laghu and Guru, while the Uddiṣṭa method converts such sequences back into numerical positions.

These methods together create a complete computational system for handling poetic patterns.

Pingala’s representation of Laghu and Guru is often compared to a binary system:
Laghu = 1
Guru = 0

Although not exactly the same as modern binary, it reflects an early form of digital and algorithmic thinking.

His work also involves combinatorics, where all possible patterns of syllables can be generated systematically.

Pingala’s contributions connect language, mathematics, and logic, making his work highly important in both literature and science.

How to Analyze Chandas (Meter)

In Sanskrit prosody, each verse (shloka) is divided into 4 parts called pāda. Each pāda contains a fixed number of syllables (aksharas).

Common meters:
• Anuṣṭubh → 8 syllables per pāda (8×4 = 32)
• Triṣṭubh → 11 syllables per pāda (11×4 = 44)
• Jagatī → 12 syllables per pāda (12×4 = 48)

Steps:
1. Convert shloka into Laghu (L) and Guru (G)
2. Count total syllables
3. Divide into 4 equal pādas
4. Identify meter based on syllable count

Examples (Divided into Pādas)

Example 1
आप्यायस्व समेतु ते विश्वतः सोम वृष्णियम्।
भवा वाजस्य संगथे॥
👉 Pāda split:
आप्यायस्व समेतु ते | विश्वतः सोम वृष्णियम् |
भवा वाजस्य संगथे | —
✅ Anuṣṭubh (8×4)
