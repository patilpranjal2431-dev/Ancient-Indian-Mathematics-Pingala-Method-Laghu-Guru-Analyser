Shloka to Laghu-Guru Conversion

Verses in Sanskrit are built from syllables (akshara). The study of how these syllables are arranged is called Chandas (prosody). Every syllable is either Laghu (L), light, or Guru (G), heavy.

Laghu — the vowel is short: अ, इ, उ, ऋ, or the matras ि, ु, ृ (or no matra, which carries the short अ), and nothing below makes it heavy.

Guru — any one of these is enough:
1. The vowel is long: आ, ई, ऊ, ॠ, ए, ऐ, ओ, औ, or the matras ा, ी, ू, ॄ, े, ै, ो, ौ. (ए and ओ are always long.)
2. The syllable carries anusvāra (ं) or visarga (ः).
3. The syllable is followed by a conjunct, meaning two or more consonants before the next vowel. This also works across a word boundary, so धर्मस्य makes the syllable before स्य heavy.
4. It is the last syllable and is closed by a consonant with halant (e.g. the म् in यम्).

Steps: (1) split the verse into syllables by their vowels, ignoring spaces and daṇḍas; (2) decide Laghu or Guru for each using the rules above; (3) write the pattern; (4) write the binary form with Laghu = 1 and Guru = 0.

Example: यदा यदा हि धर्मस्य

Syllables: य | दा | य | दा | हि | धर् | म | स्य Pattern: L G L G L G G L (दा, दा = long ā; धर् = closed by र्; म = followed by the conjunct स्य) Binary: 1 0 1 0 1 0 0 1 Uddiṣṭa row number of this pattern: 150

Spaces are ignored. Only syllables are counted, not words.

Nāṣṭa Method

Nāṣṭa turns a row number into a Laghu-Guru sequence of a given length. Start from the row number n and repeat once per syllable:

n is odd → write Guru (0), then n = (n + 1) / 2
n is even → write Laghu (1), then n = n / 2

Valid row numbers for a sequence of length L are 1 to 2L. Row 1 is all Guru and row 2L is all Laghu.

Example: row 37, length 6

37 is odd → 0 (G) → (37 + 1) / 2 = 19 19 is odd → 0 (G) → (19 + 1) / 2 = 10 10 is even → 1 (L) → 10 / 2 = 5 5 is odd → 0 (G) → (5 + 1) / 2 = 3 3 is odd → 0 (G) → (3 + 1) / 2 = 2 2 is even → 1 (L) → 2 / 2 = 1

Final sequence: 001001 = G G L G G L

All rows for length 2: row 1 = GG, row 2 = LG, row 3 = GL, row 4 = LL. This lists every possible pattern exactly once, which is early combinatorics.

Uddiṣṭa Method

Uddiṣṭa is the exact reverse of Nāṣṭa: it turns a Laghu-Guru sequence back into its row number.

Start with 1 and read the sequence from right to left (last syllable first):

Laghu (1) → ×2
Guru (0) → ×2 − 1

Example: 001001 (the result of the Nāṣṭa example)

Start: 1 6th digit 1 → 1 × 2 = 2 5th digit 0 → 2 × 2 − 1 = 3 4th digit 0 → 3 × 2 − 1 = 5 3rd digit 1 → 5 × 2 = 10 2nd digit 0 → 10 × 2 − 1 = 19 1st digit 0 → 19 × 2 − 1 = 37

Final result: 37, which brings us back to the Nāṣṭa input.

Note on trailing zeros: Guru at the end of the sequence is processed first, and 1 × 2 − 1 = 1, so it changes nothing. That is why 1010100 gives the same row number as 10101: both give 22.

Pingala and Ancient Indian Mathematics

Pingala was an ancient Indian scholar who is believed to have lived around 200 BCE. He is one of the earliest known thinkers to apply mathematical principles to linguistic and poetic analysis.

He is best known for his work Chandaḥśāstra, a foundational text on Sanskrit prosody (Chandas), which studies the structure and rhythm of poetic meters.

In this work, Pingala analyzed how Laghu (short) and Guru (long) syllables combine to form structured patterns in Sanskrit verses.

To systematically study these patterns, he introduced important algorithms such as Nāṣṭa and Uddiṣṭa.

The Nāṣṭa method converts numbers into sequences of Laghu and Guru, while the Uddiṣṭa method converts such sequences back into numerical positions.

These methods together create a complete computational system for handling poetic patterns.

This toolkit represents Laghu and Guru in the way often compared to a binary system:
Laghu = 1
Guru = 0

Although not exactly the same as modern binary, it reflects an early form of digital and algorithmic thinking.

His work also involves combinatorics, where all possible patterns of syllables can be generated systematically.

Pingala’s contributions connect language, mathematics, and logic, making his work highly important in both literature and science.

How to Analyze Chandas (Meter)

A shloka is divided into parts called pāda. Each pāda has a fixed number of syllables. This tool identifies a meter from the total syllable count:

MeterPādas × syllablesTotal
Gāyatrī3 × 824
Anuṣṭubh4 × 832
Triṣṭubh4 × 1144
Jagatī4 × 1248

Steps:
1. Convert the shloka into Laghu (L) and Guru (G)
2. Count the total syllables
3. Match the total to the table above
4. Split the pattern into pādas of the matching size

This is an approximation. It checks the syllable count only, not the Laghu-Guru pattern inside each pāda. Real Vedic verses can differ by a syllable or two, and prose will not match any meter.
